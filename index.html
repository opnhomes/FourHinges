The name

Why “Four Hinges”?

cardo, cardinis (n.)

Latin: a hinge; the pivot on which something turns.

cardinalis (adj.)

That on which something hinges; principal.

A door can be beautiful, heavy, even carved with care, but it is only as good as its hinges. Take them away and it cannot open, cannot close, cannot do the one thing a door is for.

For more than two thousand years, thinkers have noticed that a good life works the same way. Plato named four virtues in the Republic. St. Ambrose is credited with first calling them cardinal, and St. Augustine and St. Thomas Aquinas built on them. The Catechism of the Catholic Church puts it simply: “four virtues play a pivotal role and accordingly are called ‘cardinal.’” Every other virtue is grouped around them.

Long before any of them, Scripture had already listed the four together:

“And if a man love justice: her labours have great virtues; for she teacheth temperance, and prudence, and justice, and fortitude, which are such things as men can have nothing more profitable in life.”

WISDOM 8:7 · DOUAY-RHEIMS

The four virtues

A virtue is a firm habit of doing good. These four are the ones the rest depend on.

I · Prudentia

Prudence

Right reason applied to action.

Prudence sees things as they really are, takes good counsel, judges well, and then actually decides. The Catechism calls it the “charioteer of the virtues” because it steers the other three, telling courage when to stand and temperance how much is enough.

Symbol
The mirror: know yourself and see clearly
Too little
Rashness, carelessness, indecision
Counterfeit
Cunning: cleverness aimed at the wrong end
At home
Pause, ask advice, and then make the call

II · Iustitia

Justice

Giving God and neighbor what is their due.

Justice is the steady will to give each person what belongs to them: the truth, a kept promise, a fair wage, honor, attention. Toward God it is called the virtue of religion. In a family it looks like fairness among siblings and respect for parents.

Symbol
The scales: weigh fairly, favor no one
Opposed by
Taking what is owed to others: their goods, time, name, or truth
Completed by
Mercy, which gives more than is owed
At home
Keep your word, pay what you owe, tell the truth

III · Fortitudo

Fortitude

Firmness in difficulty; constancy in pursuing the good.

Fortitude is courage with a reason. It has two acts: to take on what is hard, and to endure what cannot be avoided. St. Thomas thought enduring was the greater of the two. It is the virtue of the parent up at 3 a.m. and of the one who stays the course when quitting would be easier.

Symbol
The column: it bears weight without moving
Too little
Cowardice, giving up
Too much
Recklessness: daring with no good end
At home
Do the hard right thing and finish what you start

IV · Temperantia

Temperance

Moderation in the use of good things.

Temperance keeps our appetites, for food, drink, comfort, screens, and praise, in their proper place, so that we enjoy good things without being ruled by them. It is not a dislike of pleasure. It is freedom: being the master of your desires rather than their servant.

Symbol
Two vessels: water poured to temper the wine
Too little
Indulgence, excess, being ruled by craving
Too much
Joyless refusal of the good God gives
At home
Fast now and then; put the phone down at the table

Hinge & door

Hinges hold a door. The door opens onto something.

The cardinal virtues are human virtues. They are built the way any habit is built: by practice, by small choices repeated until doing good becomes second nature. Anyone of good will can grow in them.

But hinges are not the point of a door. In the Catholic tradition, the cardinal virtues are purified and raised up by grace, and they are given their final direction by three more: faith, hope and charity. These theological virtues are gifts of God, not achievements, and they open the door onto Him.

Faith

We believe in God and all He has revealed.

Hope

We desire heaven and trust His help to reach it.

Charity

We love God above all, and our neighbor for His sake.
